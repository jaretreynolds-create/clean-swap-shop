What’s the real issue with paper towels?

Paper towels are not dangerous. They are convenient, and a roll under the sink for emergencies is fine. The clean-living question is mostly about the habit: how much single-use paper the kitchen goes through, and what comes with it:

  • Single-use by design. Every wipe is a new sheet. A busy fall kitchen can empty rolls quickly, and the restock never ends.
  • Bleached fibers. Many conventional paper towels are bleached to get that bright white. Some households would rather skip it for food-prep surfaces.
  • Additives and packaging. Many towels are treated with wet-strength additives to hold together when wet, and multipacks typically arrive wrapped in plastic.
  • Hard to recycle. Once a sheet is soaked with food or grease, it generally goes in the trash, not the recycling bin.

You don’t need to hide the paper towels tonight. Keep a roll for the messy jobs, and move the everyday wipes to cloth.

How to switch (step by step)

FAQ

Do Swedish dishcloths get smelly?

Any damp cloth can sour if it stays wet. Swedish dishcloths dry quickly when hung or laid flat, which helps. Rinse well after each use and wash them regularly according to the listing’s care notes.

How do I clean them?

Most can be rinsed by hand, machine washed, or placed on the top rack of the dishwasher. Check the care instructions on the specific listing and skip fabric softener, which can reduce absorbency.

How long does one cloth last?

With regular use and washing, a cloth typically lasts months, not days. Replace it when it gets thin, torn, or stops rinsing clean.

Are they compostable?

The brand describes its cellulose and cotton cloths as commercially compostable. Composting options vary by area, so check what your local program or backyard pile accepts.

Can they replace my kitchen sponge too?

For wiping and light scrubbing, many people use them instead of a sponge. For baked-on messes, pair the cloth with a scrub brush.
