Nonstick (Teflon/PFAS) vs Lodge Cast Iron

A calm, practical guide to why scratched nonstick pans worry so many families — and how seasoned cast iron becomes naturally slick without forever-chemical coatings.

Nonstick pans sell a dream: eggs that slide, cleanup that takes ten seconds, no skill required. The tradeoff lives in the coating. Traditional Teflon-style surfaces use PTFE, part of the broader PFAS family of “forever chemicals.” When pans overheat or the coating scratches, that dream gets complicated — fumes, flakes, and a product you replace every few years. Lodge cast iron is the opposite bet: heavier, skill-rewarding, and built to last generations once you learn a simple seasoning rhythm.

What’s the concern with nonstick coatings?

PFAS is a large class of fluorine-based chemicals that resist heat, water, and oil — useful in industry, stubborn in the environment and the body. In cookware conversations, people usually mean:

  • PTFE (Teflon-type coatings). The slick layer on classic nonstick pans. It is generally considered stable at normal cooking temps, but empty pans on high heat can exceed safe ranges and release fumes. Bird owners have long been warned about “Teflon toxicosis” from overheated coatings.
  • PFOA and related processing aids. Older manufacturing used PFOA, a PFAS linked to health and environmental harm. Many brands phased PFOA out of production, but “PFOA-free” is not the same as “PFAS-free coating.” PTFE itself is still a fluoropolymer.
  • Scratches and flakes. Metal utensils, dishwashers, and stacked pans damage coatings. Flaking means you are cooking on a compromised surface — and shopping for a replacement again.

You do not need a chemistry degree to act on this. If your pan is scratched, warped, or you routinely crank burners on empty nonstick, it is a good candidate for retirement.

Why Lodge cast iron as the clean swap

Lodge is widely available, pre-seasoned, and priced for real households — not just specialty cookware collectors. Cast iron’s “nonstick” is a thin layer of polymerized oil you build and maintain. That means:

  • No PTFE layer to scratch into your scrambled eggs
  • Oven-safe searing and baking in one pan
  • A tool that gets better with use instead of worse
  • Fewer replacements, less waste, more heirloom energy

Yes, it is heavier. Yes, you should not soak it overnight like a soup pot. Those are skills, not dealbreakers — and most people get comfortable within two weeks of weeknight cooking.

How to switch (step by step)

  1. Retire damaged nonstick. If the coating is peeling, stop using it for food. Recycle per local metal/pan guidance when possible.
  2. Order a Lodge skillet. A 10- or 12-inch covers most homes: Shop Lodge cast iron.
  3. Wash once, dry hard. Hot water, mild brush, dry on a low burner until fully dry so rust never starts.
  4. Cook fatty foods first. Bacon, roasted veggies with oil, grilled cheese — early seasoning builders. Avoid long simmered tomato sauces in brand-new pans until the seasoning deepens.
  5. Clean with care. Hot water + brush or chainmail scrubber. A little kosher salt as a scrub is fine. Thin coat of oil after drying if the pan looks dry or dull.
  6. Learn heat patience. Preheat medium, then add oil, then food. Eggs stick less when the pan is properly heated and greased — same truth as stainless, different vibe.

Nonstick convenience is a coating story. Cast iron is a material-and-maintenance story. Always follow Lodge’s current care card for the exact skillet you buy, and keep plastic utensils away from screaming-hot empty pans of any kind.

Who this swap is (and isn’t) for

Great fit: families reducing PFAS exposure, home cooks who sear and bake, anyone tired of replacing flaking pans, and people who like tools that improve with ritual.

Maybe keep one alternative: if you need feather-light cookware for a tiny induction travel setup, look at quality stainless or carbon steel rather than defaulting back to scratched PTFE. Cast iron remains the workhorse for home ranges.

FAQ

Is modern “PFOA-free” nonstick safe?

PFOA-free addresses one infamous processing chemical. Many pans still use PTFE (a PFAS polymer) as the slick coating. If your goal is avoiding fluorinated coatings altogether, cast iron, carbon steel, stainless, and ceramic (with its own caveats) are clearer paths.

Will eggs always stick on cast iron?

Not once the seasoning is solid and you preheat + oil correctly. Early weeks may take practice. Butter or oil helps. A well-loved Lodge often releases eggs beautifully.

Can I use soap?

A little mild soap is fine on modern seasoned pans when needed. The old “never soap” myth comes from harsher soaps that stripped seasoning. Dry thoroughly afterward.

What about rust?

Rust means moisture sat too long. Scrub it off, dry on heat, oil lightly, and keep going. Surface rust is repairable — unlike a flaking PTFE pan.

Is the iron leaching a problem?

Cast iron can contribute small amounts of dietary iron, especially with acidic foods. For most people that is neutral or helpful. If you have a clinical reason to limit iron, ask your clinician and prefer stainless for acidic simmer sauces.
