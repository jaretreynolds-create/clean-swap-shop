Plastic Cutting Board vs Teak Cutting Board

A calm, practical look at the plastic board you chop on every day, and why a solid teak board is the fall prep swap that lasts for years.

The plastic cutting board is the quiet workhorse of most kitchens: dishwasher-friendly, cheap, and covered in knife grooves by the end of its first year. Every one of those grooves is plastic that went somewhere, and researchers have found that chopping on plastic boards can shed microplastic particles into food. Fall is peak prep season, with squash, apples, onions, and holiday carving on the way. A solid teak board is the boho kitchen swap: warm wood on the counter, kind to knives, and built to last for years instead of seasons.

Why people move away from plastic boards

  • Microplastics in the prep zone. Studies have estimated that chopping on polyethylene and polypropylene boards can release microplastic particles, and every knife groove is material that left the board.
  • Grooves that are hard to clean. Deeply scarred plastic can trap food residue, which is why many guidelines say to replace worn plastic boards.
  • Short lifespan. Warped, stained, and grooved boards mean a new one in the cart every year or two.

You don’t have to throw your plastic board out tonight. Plenty of families keep one for raw meat and let wood handle everything else.

Teak is dense, moisture-resistant, and naturally oily, so it resists warping and is gentle on knife edges. How to switch (step by step)

  1. Pick your size. A large board covers everyday prep and holiday carving; smaller teak boards work for fruit and herbs.
  2. Order the board. Use our tagged Amazon link for the current listing: Shop the Teakhaus Teak Cutting Board.
  3. Rinse before first use. The board arrives pre-oiled. Rinse, dry, and you’re ready.
  4. Hand wash, never soak. Warm soapy water, rinse, towel dry, and stand it on its edge. Keep it out of the dishwasher.
  5. Oil monthly. Rub in food-grade mineral oil or a board cream when the wood looks dry.
  6. Reassign the plastic board. Keep it for raw meat if you like, or retire it to craft or garage duty.

FAQ

Is wood as sanitary as plastic?

Well-maintained hardwood boards are widely used at home and clean up well with hot soapy water and thorough drying. Many cooks keep a separate board for raw meat. Replace any board that gets deep cracks.

Why teak instead of bamboo or maple?

Teak is dense and naturally oily, which helps it resist moisture and warping. Bamboo boards are often glued strips and can be harder on knives; maple is a great option too. Teak is simply the low-fuss pick we like.

Can it go in the dishwasher?

No. Heat and long soaks can crack or warp wood. Hand wash, towel dry, and stand it upright.

How often do I oil it?

Roughly once a month, or whenever the wood looks pale and dry. Use food-grade mineral oil or a board cream.

Is the board heavy?

Yes. The large board weighs around 12 pounds per the listing, which is why it stays put while you chop. Choose a smaller size if you need to move it often.
